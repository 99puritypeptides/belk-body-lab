A personal training package in 2026 costs roughly $600 to $1,080 for four weeks, $1,120 to $2,040 for eight weeks, and $1,440 to $2,800 for twelve weeks of one-on-one sessions, with small-group packages running about 60% less. Longer packages lower the per-session rate. Twelve weeks remains the shortest block that reliably produces visible body-composition change.

Key Takeaways

  • Per-session price falls as the package gets longer. A 4-week block usually prices out around $75–$90 per session, while a 12-week block drops the same trainer to $60–$78 per session.
  • Four weeks changes your technique; twelve weeks changes your body. Early strength gains come mostly from neural adaptation, so the first month makes you better at lifting long before it makes you look different.
  • Tier pricing tracks trainer attention, not equipment. One-on-one costs the most because you own the hour outright, semi-private splits that hour across two to six people, and online coaching removes the hour entirely and charges monthly instead.
  • The most expensive mistake is buying too short. Clients who buy four weeks to "try it out" almost always quit right as their programming was about to get interesting, then pay full price again three months later.
  • Pick length by goal, then pick tier by budget. Decide how many weeks your goal genuinely needs first, and only then decide whether you can afford those weeks one-on-one, semi-private, or online.

Why Personal Training Is Sold in Packages, Not Just Sessions

People ask me this in the same breath as "can I just buy one session and see how it goes?" You can. Nearly every trainer in Charleston will sell you a single session, and mine runs $95 flat.

But almost nobody who buys one session gets what they actually came for. That isn't a sales line. It's a structural problem with how training works.

A single session buys you an assessment and a workout. It does not buy you a program. Programming is the thing that changes your body, and programming only functions in blocks — a base phase to build tolerance, a progression phase to add load, then a consolidation phase to lock the gains in. Chop that into a one-off and you have paid $95 for a very thorough gym tour.

So packages exist for several honest reasons, plus one that's less flattering:

  • Programming continuity. I can only progressively overload you if I know you'll be there in week six to receive the overload.
  • Scheduling. A package reserves recurring slots on my calendar, which is the only reason 6 a.m. Tuesdays exist as a reliable thing.
  • Volume pricing. Buying 24 sessions at once lowers my admin and no-show risk, so I pass a chunk of that back to you.
  • Commitment. Money already spent gets used. Money not yet spent gets rationalized away on a rainy February morning.
  • Predictable revenue. Trainers like packages because they smooth out income, and I'll say that out loud rather than pretend otherwise. It also means a trainer under cash-flow pressure has an incentive to sell you longer than you need — which is exactly why the pricing-sheet questions further down matter.

If you're still trying to work out general per-session and per-month ranges, start with my cost overview of what a personal trainer charges. This post assumes you already know the ballpark and now want to compare lengths and tiers against each other.

Package Pricing by Length: 4-Week vs 8-Week vs 12-Week

Here's the part most personal training price lists bury. The headline number on a package tells you almost nothing until you divide it by the session count.

The per-session rate is the number that matters

Two Charleston trainers can both advertise "$1,400 packages" and offer completely different value. One is selling 16 sessions at $87.50. The other is selling 24 sessions at $58.33. Same sticker, wildly different math.

So before you compare anything else, do the division. Then compare per-session rates across the lengths a trainer offers, because the spread between their shortest and longest package tells you how they actually think about commitment.

What's Actually Included at Each Tier

Length answers "how long." Tier answers "how much of me do you get." Those are separate purchases, and conflating them is how people end up disappointed by a package that was priced perfectly fairly.

Most trainers run some version of a three-tier structure. Names differ — Starter, Transform, Elite is the framing I use — but the ladder is consistent across the industry.

What You Get Starter Transform Elite
Custom programming Proven template adapted to your equipment and injury history Fully custom, rebuilt every 4 weeks around your logged numbers Fully custom and periodized across a 12-week block, adjusted weekly
Nutrition coaching Macro targets plus a grocery framework Macros, meal structure, and monthly revisions as weight moves Weekly adjustments, plus restaurant, travel, and holiday game plans
Check-in frequency Monthly form-video review Weekly video check-in with written feedback Weekly live call plus a mid-week metrics review
Message support Email, 48-hour reply window In-app messaging, same-day on weekdays Direct text, same-day, seven days a week
Price $75/month $199/month $349/month

Notice what does not change across those tiers: the quality of the programming logic. A Starter client gets a genuinely good program. What a Starter client doesn't get is me watching them execute it and adjusting on Wednesday because Tuesday went sideways.

That distinction is the whole tier ladder. You are buying attention density, not better exercises. Anyone selling you "advanced movements" at the top tier is selling you novelty, and novelty is not a training variable that matters.

Why 12 Weeks Is the Minimum Effective Dose

I don't sell 12-week packages because 12 is a satisfying number. I sell them because shorter blocks keep running out right before the interesting part.

Your first month is mostly nervous system

The classic work here is Moritani and deVries, who tracked strength gain across an eight-week training program and found that neural factors drove most of the early strength increase, with hypertrophy only becoming the dominant contributor after roughly the first three to five weeks. Translated: in month one, you get stronger largely because your brain learns to recruit muscle you already had.

That's real progress. It just isn't the progress most people are paying for. Nobody books a trainer because they want improved motor unit recruitment. They book because they want their jeans to fit differently, and that requires tissue change, which starts showing up after the neural window closes.

The research itself is built on longer blocks

When ACSM published its 2026 position stand on resistance training prescription, the review team synthesized 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. The eligibility criteria are the tell: trials had to run at least six weeks, and the included range stretched from six to fifty-two weeks.

Exercise scientists don't bother measuring four-week interventions in body-composition research, because four weeks doesn't produce a signal worth measuring. That should tell you something about a four-week transformation package.

Fat loss has a speed limit

The CDC's guidance is that people who lose weight gradually and steadily, around one to two pounds per week, are more likely to keep it off. Run the arithmetic on a four-week package and your ceiling is roughly four to eight pounds, some of which is water and glycogen.

Over twelve weeks, that same safe rate compounds to twelve to twenty-four pounds. Same weekly effort. Wildly different outcome, purely because you let the math run.

Habits take longer than anyone wants them to

There's also the behavior side. In the most-cited real-world study on this, Lally and colleagues found that automaticity took an average of 66 days to plateau, with individual times ranging from 18 to 254 days.

Sixty-six days is roughly nine and a half weeks. A four-week package ends before the average person's new behavior has become automatic, which is precisely why so many people finish a short package feeling good and then drift within a month.

I've never had a client tell me they regretted buying twelve weeks. I've had plenty tell me they regretted buying four, usually about six weeks later when they realized they'd stopped right as things started working.

Group / Semi-Private Package Rates

Group personal training rates confuse people because "group training" covers three genuinely different products at three different prices.

  • Semi-private (2–3 people): $35–$50 per person, per session. Everyone runs an individual program. I'm rotating between you, and you still get hands-on correction on the lifts that need it.
  • Small group (4–6 people): $25–$35 per person, per session. One shared program with individual load prescriptions. Less correction per person, considerably more energy in the room.
  • Large group or bootcamp (8–12 people): $18–$28 per person, per session. One program, minimal individualization, and honestly closer to a class than to training.

Package math follows the same length logic as one-on-one. A 12-week semi-private package at two sessions per week works out to 24 sessions, so roughly $840 to $1,200 at $35–$50 per session. The same 12 weeks in a small group lands closer to $600–$840.

Some trainers also run open-ended small-group memberships at $149–$249 per month for a fixed number of weekly slots. That model works well for people who already know how to train and mostly need the appointment on the calendar.

Who I actually steer toward semi-private

Semi-private is not the budget consolation prize. For a decent chunk of my clients it's the better product, and I say so during consults even though it prices lower.

It works particularly well for people who train harder with witnesses, for couples and friend pairs with similar schedules, and for anyone who has already spent a block one-on-one and doesn't need constant technical correction anymore. It works badly for people with significant movement restrictions, for anyone rehabbing something, and for clients who go quiet and coast the second attention moves elsewhere.

The Real Cost-Per-Result Math

Here's the argument I make in consults, and the reason I'd rather someone buy twelve weeks of semi-private than four weeks one-on-one.

Take a composite scenario: a 175-pound office worker in Mount Pleasant who wants to drop about fifteen pounds. Assume a safe rate of 1 to 1.5 pounds per week, consistent with CDC guidance. Then compare three packages on cost per pound.

8 weeks, 1-on-1 (16 sessions @ $85) $1,360 8 ~10 lb $136 ~6 lb $227
12 weeks, 1-on-1 (24 sessions @ $75) $1,800 12 ~15 lb $120 ~12.75 lb $141

*Retention percentages here (40%, 60%, 85%) are my own working assumptions from coaching, not published data. Plug in your own if you think I'm being generous to the 12-week column — the ordering holds unless you assume short packages stick better than long ones, which nothing I've seen supports.

The headline is that the cheap package is only cheap at checkout. At $720 the four-week block is the easiest yes in the room. Twelve months later it's frequently the most expensive line item, because most of those people buy again.

I've watched this loop run dozens of times. Someone buys four weeks in January, feels great, stops in February, comes back in June, and buys four weeks again. That's $1,440 across two short blocks with nothing compounding between them — more than the twelve-week package they turned down for being too expensive.

How to Read a Personal Training Pricing Sheet

Use this on any trainer's pricing sheet, mine included. A legitimate personal training price list discloses all six of these without you having to ask twice.

  1. Per-session cost, shown or easily calculated. Total price divided by session count should be printed on the sheet. If it isn't, ask, and note how quickly they answer.
  2. Session length and what counts as a session. Fifty minutes and sixty minutes are different products at the same price. Also ask whether an assessment, a check-in call, or a form-video review burns a session.
  3. Expiration window. Every package should have one, and it should be reasonable. A 24-session package with a 90-day window is normal; the same package with a 45-day window is designed for you to forfeit sessions.
  4. What's included beyond the hour. Programming, nutrition guidance, check-ins, and messaging either come with it or they don't. Vague answers here usually mean "they don't."
  5. Cancellation, reschedule, and no-show policy. Twenty-four hours is the industry standard. What matters more is whether the policy is written down, because unwritten policies get enforced inconsistently and always in the trainer's favor.
  6. Refund, transfer, pause, and financing terms. Life happens — job relocations, pregnancies, surgeries. Ask what happens to unused sessions. If payment plans are offered, ask what the plan totals versus paying upfront, since the difference is effectively an interest rate.

One more thing worth checking, which isn't a line item: whether the trainer holds a current certification from an NCCA-accredited body such as NASM, ACSM, or NSCA, and whether they carry liability insurance. Both are easy to verify and neither should be awkward to ask about.

How to Choose the Right Package for Your Goal

Decide length first. Length is set by physiology and by your goal, and no budget conversation changes how long adaptation takes. Then decide tier, because tier is where your budget actually has room to move.

Here's the framework I run in consults:

  • "I want to learn to lift safely" or "I'm coming back after a long layoff" → 4–6 weeks → one-on-one, Starter coaching. Short is genuinely fine here, because the goal is skill acquisition and skill comes fast.
  • "I want to lose 10–20 pounds" → 12 weeks minimum → one-on-one or semi-private, Transform. Anything shorter caps your result below what you came for.
  • "I want to look different in photos" or "I have an event" → 12–16 weeks → Transform or Elite, depending on how fixed the date is. Count backwards from the event and add two weeks of buffer.
  • "I already train, I just need programming and accountability" → 8–12 weeks → online Starter or small group. You're buying direction, not supervision.
  • "I finished a transformation and want to hold it" → open-ended monthly → small group or Starter. This is the one case where the no-end-date structure is the right structure.

If your budget can't cover twelve weeks one-on-one, don't buy four weeks one-on-one. Buy twelve weeks semi-private or twelve weeks online instead. Longer at a lower tier beats shorter at a higher tier essentially every time, and it isn't close.
