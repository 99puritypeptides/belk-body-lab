Why Myrtle Beach Clients Are Choosing Online Personal Training

The Grand Strand runs on visitor schedules, not gym schedules

The scale here is genuinely unusual. Tourism Works for the Grand Strand reports 18.2 million visitors and $13.3 billion in visitor spending in 2025, supporting more than 82,000 tourism-related jobs across the region.

Now, I want to correct a lazy assumption while I'm here. People outside the area assume everyone in Myrtle Beach works six months a year. Coastal Carolina University economists estimate about 80% of local tourism jobs are year-round and full-time, so that story is mostly wrong.

The seasonality that actually matters for training isn't employment. It's the shape of your week. Restaurant closes at eleven. Front desk turns over at seven. Golf ops start before sunrise from March through May. Housekeeping and event staff pick up doubles the week of the Fourth. A trainer who only has 6 a.m. and 5 p.m. slots is useless to half the workforce here.

Online coaching removes the appointment entirely. Your session happens when your day allows, not when my calendar does.

Peak season makes local gyms harder to use

Here's what clients tell me every June. The squat racks fill up with vacationers, the condo and resort gyms get slammed, and a 45-minute lift stretches to 70 minutes of waiting around. Meanwhile the traffic on Highway 17 turns a ten-minute drive into thirty.

Because your program lives in an app, we work around that. If your gym is packed, I've already built you an alternate session using what's free. If you're stuck at a condo gym with dumbbells up to 50 pounds, your program uses dumbbells up to 50 pounds.

Specialized coaching is thin outside the peak months

I won't pretend to have counted every trainer in Horry County. What I can tell you is what clients report: plenty of general trainers, far fewer coaches who specialize in body recomposition, and real turnover between seasons. Starting over with a new trainer in month four kills more transformations than bad programming does.

Remote coaching gives you one coach for the whole build. That continuity matters more than proximity.

How Online Personal Training Actually Works, Step by Step

People picture online coaching as a template emailed once a month. That's not coaching, that's a document. Here's the actual process I run.

  1. Intake and consult call. We talk for 30 to 45 minutes by phone or video. I ask about your training history, injuries, current schedule, what equipment you can reach, how you eat on a work night versus a day off, and what you actually want in 12 weeks. I also ask what's failed before, because that's usually the most useful answer.
  2. Custom program build. I build your training block around your real week, not an ideal one. A four-day plan doesn't help a banquet manager in July. If you can commit to three sessions, we build three that work, plus a 25-minute backup session for the days that fall apart.
  3. App delivery. Your program lands in the coaching app with video demonstrations for every lift, prescribed sets, reps, rest and effort targets. You open it at the gym, hit start, and log your weights as you go.
  4. Weekly check-ins. Once a week you send in your numbers: body weight trend, progress photos if you're using them, training logs, energy, sleep, and how the week actually went. It takes about five minutes on your end.
  5. Adjustments. I review the check-in and change the plan. Sometimes that's adding load, sometimes it's cutting volume because you worked 55 hours, sometimes it's fixing a nutrition target that isn't landing. Then the updated week goes live in the app.

That loop repeats. Nothing about it requires us to stand in the same building.

Online vs In-Person: Is It Actually As Effective?

This is the objection I get most, and it deserves a real answer instead of a sales line.

What the research shows

A 2024 randomized trial in the International Journal of Exercise Science ran physically inactive adults through 36 identical sessions over three months. One group trained remotely by video call, one trained in person, and a control group didn't train. Both training groups significantly improved body fat percentage, muscle percentage, visceral fat, cholesterol markers and grip strength. When researchers compared the two, results were similar across the board except blood glucose, where the in-person group did better. Worth noting: the sample was only 30 people, so I'd call that supportive evidence rather than settled proof.

A larger 2025 trial in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research is more useful for the accountability question. Researchers put 79 trained adults through ten weeks of full-body lifting under three conditions: in-person supervision, app-guided coaching, or a self-guided PDF. Adherence came out at 88.2% supervised, 81.2% app-guided, and 52.2% self-guided.

Read those numbers honestly. In-person supervision won, and the supervised group showed the clearest fat-free mass gains. But app-guided coaching landed within seven points of it, while going it alone with a document collapsed to barely half the sessions completed.

The gap that decides your results isn't between a trainer standing next to you and a trainer on your phone. It's between having a coach and having a document.

Where in-person genuinely wins

I'll give the honest list. Hands-on cueing beats video for a beginner learning to hinge. Live spotting lets you push closer to failure on heavy barbell work. And some people simply need a human in the room to show up at all.

If that last one describes you, hire someone local and pay the premium. I'd rather tell you that than sell you a program you'll abandon in week three.

Where online genuinely wins

Frequency of contact, for one. A twice-weekly in-person client gets two hours of coaching a week and no input on the other 166. My online clients get programming for every session, nutrition structure for every day, and a message thread that stays open.

Cost is the other one. The same budget buys you comprehensive coverage instead of two supervised hours. For fat loss especially, that trade favors online, because fat loss is decided by what happens across the whole week.

Who Online Coaching Works Best For (and Who It Doesn't)

It works well if you're this person

You can walk into a gym, or a decent home setup, without a chaperone. You'll log your sessions honestly, including the bad ones. You can send a check-in once a week without being chased. And you want structure applied to your whole week rather than two supervised hours.

Shift workers do especially well, which is why the Grand Strand is a natural fit. So do parents with unpredictable evenings, and anyone who's already trained for a year or two and stalled out.

It doesn't work if you're this person

Be honest with yourself here, because I'd rather lose a sale than watch someone waste three months.

  • You need someone physically present to show up. If the appointment is the only reason you go, online won't fix that. Hire local.
  • You've never touched a barbell and the idea terrifies you. A handful of in-person sessions to learn the lifts first is a smarter first purchase. Come to me after.
  • You're rehabbing a fresh injury or post-surgical. That's a physical therapist's job, not a remote coach's. I'll say so on the consult call.
  • You won't message, log, or check in. Coaching runs on the information you send me. No data, no adjustments, no results.

Roughly one in ten people who reach out gets told online isn't their best option. That's a normal number, and saying it out loud is part of the job.

How to Get Started

The process is deliberately short, because long onboarding is where motivation goes to die.

  1. Send an inquiry. Tell me your goal, your schedule, and what equipment you have access to on the Grand Strand. Two paragraphs is plenty.
  2. Book the consult call. Thirty to forty-five minutes, no charge, no pitch deck. I'll tell you honestly whether online is right for you and which tier fits.
  3. Complete the intake. Training history, injury history, food preferences, work pattern, and a starting set of measurements or photos if you want them tracked.
  4. Get your program. First block lands in the app within a few days of intake, built to your equipment and your week.
  5. Train, log, check in. Then we run the loop and adjust from there.

Before your first session, do two small things: photograph the equipment rack at whatever gym you'll use, and write down your realistic training days for the next month. Those two pieces of information change your program more than anything else you can tell me.
